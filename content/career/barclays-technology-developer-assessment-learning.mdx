In 2026, I reached the final assessment stage twice through Barclays Technology Developer recruitment routes, with final assessments held at Barclays' Radbroke campus. One was the Discovery Programme; the other was the main internship application process. Neither experience made me a Barclays employee, but both influenced how I explain my technical work to people outside a research setting.

## Technical knowledge is not the whole answer

The first experience showed me that technical knowledge alone is not enough in an interview. I had spent years focusing on correct implementations and measurable results. I was comfortable explaining how a system worked, yet I needed to become better at explaining why a decision mattered to the people using it. I also needed to show that I considered other perspectives rather than only describing my preferred technical approach.

## Preparing STAR examples properly

After that process, I prepared differently. I worked on communicating the situation, the task, my actions and the result of each example without losing the practical detail. I now separate what I personally did from what the wider research team contributed, and where a result is still under investigation, I say so.

## Thinking about the bank's customers

More importantly, I asked how a similar decision would affect a bank's customers, operations and security. A security control that works in a research lab is only part of the problem. A real business also needs clear failure handling, maintainable systems and evidence that an implementation behaves as expected.

## Turning PhD work into engineering examples

My PhD offered useful examples. I had built and tested an MQTT smart-home research environment, implemented cryptographic processing and investigated network evidence. Those activities required careful debugging, repeatable testing and attention to subtle discrepancies. The interviews encouraged me to translate those abilities into terms that mattered to a wider engineering team: dependable delivery, risk awareness, communication and user impact.

## Stakeholders, alternatives and independent validation

I also learned to treat feedback as evidence. A strong explanation should acknowledge trade-offs, consider alternative approaches and describe how results were independently checked. I now try to make those elements explicit rather than assume an interviewer will infer them.

## The biggest lesson

I am sharing this as a personal reflection, not as a description of Barclays' official assessment criteria. I have not included confidential interview questions or internal recruitment information. The biggest lesson was to present the engineer behind the research, not just the research itself.
