My forensics experience comes from two different places, and it is worth being precise about each. The first is academic coursework during my MSc. The second is investigation work inside my PhD research. Neither is professional casework for a law enforcement body or a client.

<ForensicsTracks />

## Track one: MSc forensic coursework

During my MSc in Cybersecurity and Digital Forensics at the University of Huddersfield, completed with Distinction in 2024, I used three established forensic tools in practical investigation exercises set as part of the course:

- **FTK Imager**, a tool for acquiring and previewing forensic images of storage media.
- **Autopsy**, an open-source platform for analysing disk images and the files, artefacts and activity within them.
- **EnCase**, a commercial forensic suite used for acquisition and analysis.

In these exercises I examined digital evidence, documented my observations as I worked and reported my findings. It taught me to separate what the evidence shows from what I might initially suspect, an approach that continues to shape how I investigate systems and experimental data.

These were academic exercises on prepared material. They were not criminal investigations.

## Track two: network investigation in my PhD

My PhD involves investigating controlled cyberattacks on a smart-home testbed. That work uses the same habits on network evidence:

- **Capture.** Packet captures taken with tcpdump at more than one point in the system, and examined in Wireshark.
- **Logs.** Mosquitto broker logs showing connections, authentication and access-control decisions, alongside application logs from the consumer and Home Assistant.
- **Correlation.** Python scripts that match timestamps and message identifiers across captures and logs, to establish what happened to each message.
- **Integrity.** SHA-256 hashes recorded for archived evidence, so the analysed files can be confirmed to match the originals.
- **Independent checks.** Separate audit scripts that re-examine the evidence without relying on the code that produced it.

The question in each investigation is the one an incident responder asks: what actually happened? Did an attack message reach the broker, was it rejected, did the application act on it, and what effect did it have on legitimate traffic?

## Why it matters for forensic and incident response roles

The coursework gave me the tools and the procedure. The PhD work gave me months of practice reconstructing events from imperfect evidence, and an understanding of where each source of evidence stops being reliable. A packet seen on one interface does not prove an application processed it, and a log entry is only as trustworthy as the system that wrote it.
