Picture a smart thermostat bought in a UK shop in 2027. Its box promises security updates for five years. The heating works, so nobody replaces it, and in 2037 it is still on the wall, still connected and still trusting whatever firmware arrives with a valid signature.

That thermostat sits at the intersection of two UK policy priorities: securing consumer smart devices today and preparing digital systems for post-quantum cryptography. This brief argues that the connection between them deserves greater attention.

## Two policies, one timeline

| Date | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 29 April 2024 | The UK product security regime under the PSTI Act 2022 comes into force |
| 11 September 2026 | Reporting obligations under the EU Cyber Resilience Act begin to apply |
| 11 December 2027 | The Cyber Resilience Act's main obligations apply to products sold in the EU |
| 2028 | NCSC target for discovery and an initial post-quantum migration plan |
| 2031 | NCSC target for the highest-priority migrations |
| 2035 | NCSC target for completing migration to post-quantum cryptography |

A device sold during the next few years will spend much of its working life inside the NCSC's migration window. Today's product rules say very little about how it will get through that window.

## What the UK rules require today

The Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure Act 2022, with its 2023 regulations, sets three baseline requirements for consumer connectable products sold in the UK:

1. No universal default or easily guessable passwords.
2. A published way to report security issues.
3. A published minimum period during which the product will receive security updates.

These were the right first steps. They target the failures behind many real attacks on home devices, and they are simple enough to enforce. They draw on a wider European standard, ETSI EN 303 645, which contains further provisions beyond the three mandatory requirements. None of the three requirements mentions cryptography directly.

## The update that has to ship in time

Most discussion of quantum risk focuses on confidentiality: "harvest now, decrypt later". For banks and government organisations, protecting the long-term confidentiality of sensitive information is a major concern. For smart homes, authenticity deserves at least as much attention.

Here is why. Some smart-home telemetry has a relatively short confidentiality lifetime, although occupancy patterns and behavioural records may remain sensitive for years. Firmware authenticity presents a different risk because a malicious update could compromise the device itself. If an attacker can sign firmware that a device accepts, they control the device. Many devices check updates with an RSA or elliptic-curve signature, and a sufficiently capable quantum computer would be able to forge those.

This creates a deadline that is easy to miss. For devices that rely exclusively on classical signatures to authorise firmware changes, moving to post-quantum verification may require a trusted update while those signatures remain secure. Devices designed with alternative trusted verification mechanisms may have other migration paths. The important point is that manufacturers should establish a secure upgrade route before existing cryptographic protections become vulnerable.

Good options already exist. NIST has approved the stateful hash-based signature schemes LMS and XMSS in SP 800-208, which are widely recommended for firmware and software signing, and has standardised the stateless SLH-DSA in FIPS 205. Their security rests on hash functions rather than factoring or discrete logarithms. They are bigger and slower in some respects, which is exactly why the decision belongs in the hardware design stage and not in a rushed update in the 2030s.

## Where my research fits

My PhD studies Paillier homomorphic encryption for privacy-preserving smart-home data, evaluated on a virtualised MQTT testbed at 2048-bit and 3072-bit key sizes. Paillier relies on composite residuosity assumptions and is not considered secure against sufficiently capable quantum computers. Lattice-based homomorphic schemes rely on mathematical problems believed to resist quantum attacks, although their computational, memory and communication requirements can present challenges for resource-constrained devices.

My VM-based MQTT testbed demonstrates the latency and throughput costs of Paillier encryption in a smart-home architecture. It does not directly measure energy consumption or performance on physical microcontrollers. Those measurements would be valuable for understanding the costs of cryptographic migration on constrained smart-home devices. Policy informed by practical testing is more likely to produce security requirements that manufacturers can realistically implement.

## Five recommendations

**1. Add a cryptographic update readiness statement.** Manufacturers already provide a statement of compliance. One more short answer would help: can this device change the algorithm it uses to verify updates, in the field, without new hardware? Yes or no, with a sentence of explanation.

**2. Expect post-quantum-capable update verification on devices sold after 2028.** This is my own policy proposal. It is not a requirement under the PSTI regime today, and it is not an NCSC requirement on consumer device manufacturers: the NCSC's 2028 milestone concerns discovery and initial migration planning. The proposal does not require every device to use post-quantum cryptography on day one. It asks that the bootloader and update path can accept a post-quantum or hash-based signature when the time comes. Applying the 2028 milestone to device design in this way would give manufacturers a clear, early target.

**3. Show support periods against the 2035 horizon.** A published support period is useful. Showing it against the NCSC's 2035 completion target makes it more useful: a buyer could see at a glance whether a device will still be supported when post-quantum migration should be finished.

**4. Prioritise firmware authenticity according to risk.** For long-lived consumer devices, quantum-resistant firmware-update verification should be a major migration priority because compromised update authentication could allow attackers to install malicious firmware. Key exchange and confidentiality protections also matter, particularly for devices handling sensitive information. Manufacturers should determine the appropriate migration order through device-specific risk assessments.

**5. Fund measurement on constrained hardware.** Post-quantum costs on servers are well studied. Costs on cheap microcontrollers, battery devices and home hubs are less understood. Small public testbeds that include physical devices, extending the kind of virtualised environment I use in my research, could give regulators and manufacturers shared evidence before rules are written.

## What this brief does not claim

This is my personal analysis, not legal advice, and I do not speak for DSIT, the Office for Product Safety and Standards, the NCSC or any manufacturer. I am not claiming that a cryptographically relevant quantum computer will exist by any particular date, or that every home device needs post-quantum cryptography now. The argument is narrower: the ability to upgrade safely has to be built in before it is needed, and the product security regime is the natural place to ask for it. Dates for the EU Cyber Resilience Act refer to obligations in the EU and are included for comparison only.
